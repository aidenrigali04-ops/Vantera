# Psychology & Framing

Use these to make a *relevant* offer easier to say yes to — never to push a wrong fit. If it's not a fit, the right move is a clean exit.

## Cialdini's 7 principles
1. Reciprocity — give before you ask. Lead with a useful insight or observation before requesting the meeting.
2. Commitment & consistency — stack micro-yeses. Get them to state the problem in their own words; they'll defend their own conclusion.
3. Social proof — peers, not famous logos. "The {role}s at companies like yours who took this call were usually wrestling with {pain}." Specific and similar beats big and famous. Never invent customers.
4. Authority — competence signals, briefly. One sharp observation that shows you understand their job beats claiming you're "the leader."
5. Liking — warmth, similarity, sincere interest. People meet with people they like.
6. Scarcity / loss aversion — the cost of inaction, not fake urgency. People are ~2x more motivated to avoid a loss than to achieve a gain. "Every month this stays manual is {implication}." Never fake "limited spots."
7. Unity — shared identity. "As someone also drowning in {shared problem}..." Being on the same side beats being across the table.

## Tactical empathy
- Labeling — name their likely emotion to defuse it: "It seems like you've had a vendor overpromise before." Naming a negative feeling shrinks it.
- Calibrated questions — open "how"/"what" questions that make them solve it: "How would your team even evaluate something like this?" They feel in control; you get commitment.
- No-oriented questions — invite "no" to create safety: "Would it be ridiculous to grab 15 minutes next week?" A "no" feels safer than being cornered into "yes."
- Accusation audit — say the bad thing first: "You're probably thinking I'm just another cold caller." Disarms the objection before it's raised.
- Mirroring — repeat their last 1–3 words as a question.

## Framing effects
- Loss frame > gain frame for urgency: "what this costs you" lands harder than "what you'd gain."
- Anchor the ask low: "just 15 minutes," "no prep," "kick me out at minute 3."
- Contrast: the alternative to a 15-minute call is living with the problem another quarter. The meeting looks easy by comparison.
- Future pacing: "picture next quarter with {pain} off your plate." Pre-living the outcome increases desire for it.
- Endowed progress: "you've clearly thought about this" positions the meeting as completing a path they're already on.

## Autonomy & reactance (why low-pressure wins)
- Push and people push back to protect their freedom. Give them the out explicitly — "you can tell me to get lost," "totally fine if it's a no." Granting freedom increases compliance.
- The magic question: "Is it a ridiculous idea to...?" / "Would you be opposed to...?" lets them say no while moving forward.
- Take-away: "This might not even be for you, but..." Removing the pressure often makes them lean in.

## Status quo & decision science
- Inertia is your real competitor. Most prospects choose nothing, not a competitor. Make doing nothing feel like the costly, risky option.
- Cost of inaction — quantify the bleed of the current state through Implication questions.
- Cognitive load — a confused mind says no. One idea per turn, one question at a time, one clear next step.
- Peak-end rule — people remember the emotional peak and the ending. End every call warm and gracious, even on a no.

## Ethical guardrails (non-negotiable)
- Persuasion ≠ manipulation. Every claim must be true. No invented proof, no false scarcity, no guilt.
- If they're not a fit, stop. These principles remove friction from a good yes, never force a bad one.
- You should be comfortable having every line played back to the prospect. If not, don't say it.
