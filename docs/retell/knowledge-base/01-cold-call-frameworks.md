# Cold-Call Frameworks

Field-tested B2B cold-call methods. The goal of a cold call is a booked meeting — never a sale on the call.

## The permission-based opener
Disarm the telemarketer reflex in the first 5 seconds.
- "Hi {First Name}, it's {Name} with {Company} — I know you weren't expecting my call. Can I have 27 seconds to tell you why I'm calling, and you decide if it's worth continuing?"
- Honesty ("you weren't expecting my call") plus an explicit out ("you decide") lowers defenses.
- Oddly specific numbers (27 seconds, not "a minute") signal a real human.

## The "bad time?" inversion
- "Did I catch you at a bad time?" beats "Do you have a few minutes?"
- A no-oriented question gives the prospect control. People say "no, go ahead" far more readily than "yes" to a stranger.

## The reason-for-the-call (one sentence, about them)
Lead with their likely pain or trigger, end with the outcome — never a feature.
- Bad: "We're a platform that does X, Y, and Z."
- Good: "I'm calling because I work with {role}s, and the thing I keep hearing is {pain}. Curious if that's on your radar at all?"
- Then stop talking. The question hands them the wheel.

## SPIN questioning — diagnose before you prescribe
Move in order; the value is in Implication.
1. Situation — light context: "How are you handling {area} today?"
2. Problem — surface a difficulty: "Where does that break down?"
3. Implication — make the pain concrete and costly: "What does that cost you when it slips?"
4. Need-payoff — let them state the value: "If that were solved, what would it free you up to do?"

## Gap selling — sell the distance, not the product
- Establish current state (and its impact), then future state (where they want to be). The gap between them is the value.
- You never need to pitch features — point at the gap and offer the meeting as the way to close it.

## The Challenger move — teach, tailor, take control
- Teach something non-obvious about their world: "Most {role}s assume {X}, but the ones pulling ahead found {Y}."
- Tailor it to their situation.
- Take control of the next step confidently, without pushing.

## The micro-yes ladder
Stack tiny agreements so the meeting is the next small step, not a leap.
- "Makes sense?" → "Worth understanding?" → "Worth 15 minutes?"

## The assumptive, two-option close
- Ask which, not whether: "I've got Thursday at 10 or Friday at 2 — which is easier?"
- Keep the ask tiny: "15 minutes, no prep, kick me out at minute 3 if it's not relevant."

## Voicemail (under 20 seconds, curiosity-first)
- "Hi {First Name}, it's {Name} at {Company}. I had a quick thought on {pain} for your team — probably worth two minutes. Reach me at {number}. Again, {number}. Talk soon."
- Don't pitch in the voicemail. Leave an open loop, not a summary. Say the number slowly, twice.

## The real enemy
Your competitor on a cold call is rarely another vendor — it's indifference and inertia. Every method here exists to turn "meh" into mild curiosity. You're buying 15 minutes of attention, nothing more.

## Cadence reminders
- Talk ~30% of the time. Ask, then go quiet.
- One question per breath — never bundle three.
- Slow down. Rushing reads as nervous and salesy.
- Mirror their last 1–3 words as a question to keep them talking ("...too manual?").
